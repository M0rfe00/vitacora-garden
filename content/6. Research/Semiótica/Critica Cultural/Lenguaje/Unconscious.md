# Carl Jung - The Tarot and The Unconscious
___
Jung was the first psychiatrist to discover and study the collective unconscious and to attribute significant importance and meaning to it, as he established the existence of the ultimate paradox: that ==the conscious self and the unconscious exist interdependently==, In other words, ==the well being of one depends on the well being of the other==.

==If the connection between these two weakens or breaks entirely, the person suffers psychological imbalances==, and the mind, the soul, descends into chaos, through his research on his patients, Jung discovered that the main causes of this problem stemmed from the impoverishment of people’s creative spirit.

In his clinical work, Jung found evidence that ==these disorders were due to a narrowing of the psychic totality, composed of the conscious and the unconscious, in which the patient focused only on the conscious==, he also discovered that these ailments were becoming increasingly acute and frequent in modern times, and he believed this was ==because in ancient times people had much closer contact with the creative, the interpretive, and, above all, the intuitive==.

That is why he attached great therapeutic importance to all the irrational ways in which humans had sought to explore the mysteries of life in ancient times. This explains his interest in alchemy, esoteric astrology, and the tarot. Specifically, regarding the latter, he recognized that the Arcana of the tarot had their origin in the patterns of the collective unconscious—the archetypes.

Like archetypes in dreams, the Arcana carry a symbolic significance that comes from beyond the level of consciousness and ==cannot be understood in a purely rational way==. Although they have a specific symbolic meaning, ==they must be interpreted in conjunction with feeling and intuition==.



# Alchimia - The intuition in esotericism
___
Many believe that the splendor of alchemical imagery may have reached its peak in 1677 with one of the great classical works: *El Mutus Liber* (*The Silent Book*), which presents the entire Hermetic philosophy through hieroglyphic figures; as ==it contains no text==, it is called *The Silent Book*.

The natural reaction to this is to ask: ==Why read something I won’t understand at all, or only very little of?== It’s a question that even the alchemists of old found quite understandable, since no single, inflexible rule of reasoning can be applied to the reading of all alchemical texts; each alchemist seemed to follow an individual path and express their experiences in their own language and codes.

Sometimes a thousand words explain the same thing, and sometimes the same word, depending on the context, explains something else; there are even various documents that prove it was ==not unusual for one alchemist to have trouble understanding the writings of another alchemist==. For all these reasons, Nicolas Valois wrote the following sentence: “==Set aside the diversity of words, for all of these are nothing more than a single operation.==”

==Alchemists did not speak to be understood by reason; rather, they addressed deep intuition, what they called “Adamic faith,”== which lies imprisoned within human beings. For this reason, instead of words, they preferred the language of symbols and visual allegories, which engage in direct dialogue with the unconscious without needing to be consciously reasoned out.

Nor does one need to be a genius to realize that ==the treatises were intentionally complex==; yet they urged: “Read, read, and reread,” encouraging readers to persevere without succumbing to fatigue, anger, or frustration, until understanding ==eventually emerges from the unconscious into the conscious, and until the allegories are illuminated and filled with meaning.==

Thus, the statement in the "Mutus Liber" suggests that one must read it many times until, at a certain moment, one can “re-read” it, not in the sense of reading it once more, but of accessing its second reading: to connect with the subtlety of the master who wrote it and recognize its symbols; in other words, faith and perseverance were required before undertaking the "labora et invenies": “work and you will find,” that is, to begin the alchemical work.

 The result of this art was supposed to be so extraordinary, so far beyond any human logic or imagination, that it could only be revealed to those who had persevered through the deliberate darkness of the alchemical texts without losing heart.
